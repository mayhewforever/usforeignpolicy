Aim and scope

This course develops historical explanations of U.S. foreign-policy choices through domestic politics, external constraints and instruments of power.

Founding, expansion, overseas empire, world wars, Cold War, post-Cold War order, the wars after 9/11 and contemporary competition.

Five learning outcomes

  1. Explain turning points in U.S. foreign policy from the founding era to contemporary strategy.
  2. Analyse U.S. foreign-policy choices comparatively through domestic institutions, elections, political economy, public opinion, race and ideas, and the roles of international structures, allies, rivals and non-state actors.
  3. Distinguish military coercion, economic statecraft, diplomacy and soft power in U.S. foreign policy through historical cases.
  4. Evaluate primary documents and scholarly interpretations through source criticism.
  5. Defend an evidence-based argument about the consequences of U.S. foreign policy in writing and orally, accounting for competing perspectives and evidentiary limits through respectful debate.

Teaching methods

Short explanatory lectures, document workshops, structured comparisons, small-group discussion and evidence-based writing. Start with the plain explanation and key terms, then compare the detailed case and assigned source. Additional media are optional.

14 teaching weeks and 15 online modules

Weeks 1–12 follow modules with the same numbers. Week 13 combines modules 13 and 14; week 14 covers module 15. Each weekly 300–400-word analytical note includes two source references, one limitation and one proposed corroborating source.

Teaching week 1 · Module 1 · Founding a Republic in an Imperial World

American independence was an international achievement as well as a domestic revolution. The rebellion needed supplies, credit, diplomatic recognition, and ultimately French military support.

Preparation: Read Treaty of Alliance with France (1778) and Constitution of the United States (1787). Prepare a 300–400-word analytical note with two source references, one explicit limitation and one proposed corroborating source.

Document workshop: Alliance and authorization; alliance bargaining exercise. Contrast 1778 commitments with institutions authorising comparable commitments after 1787; avoid retroactive application. Role-play American negotiators, French officials and a sceptical American legislator. Identify three shared interests and two possible conflicts; justify cooperation without assuming identical values. End with a source note separating documentary evidence from inference.

Teaching week 2 · Module 2 · Continental Expansion and the Monroe Doctrine

The early republic sought security by expanding its territory and defending its commerce, but neither objective was politically neutral. The Louisiana Purchase raised constitutional questions for Jefferson and intensified questions about slavery, settlement, and Indigenous sovereignty.

Preparation: Read Louisiana Purchase Treaty (1803) and Monroe Doctrine (1823). Prepare a 300–400-word analytical note with two source references, one explicit limitation and one proposed corroborating source.

Document workshop: Declaration versus capability; instrument classification workshop. Identify what Monroe opposes, leaves untouched and whom he addresses; compare with the Louisiana Purchase transaction. Classify the purchase, a commercial restriction and the doctrine by desired outcome, audience, influence mechanism, domestic supporters and likely costs. Explain why peaceful action and soft power are not interchangeable.

Teaching week 3 · Module 3 · Manifest Destiny, Slavery, and Continental War

Expansion in the middle decades of the nineteenth century joined domestic state-building to international conflict. Indian removal, the annexation of Texas, the Oregon settlement, and war with Mexico were not disconnected events: each concerned land, sovereignty, economic opportunity, and the future political balance of the republic.

Preparation: Read Jackson’s Message on Indian Removal (1830) and Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo (1848). Prepare a 300–400-word analytical note with two source references, one explicit limitation and one proposed corroborating source.

Document workshop: Rights under unequal power; treaty and justification source clinic. Compare benefits claimed in Jackson’s message with treaty rights and obligations. Identify present and missing voices. Annotate both texts for authority, representations of affected people, benefits, protection and silences. Produce an evidence table distinguishing what the documents establish from claims requiring additional sources.

Teaching week 4 · Module 4 · Civil War Diplomacy and the Rise of Industrial Power

The Civil War placed the survival and international standing of the United States at stake. Union and Confederate leaders competed over recognition, trade, credit, and European opinion.

Preparation: Read Emancipation Proclamation (1863) and Treaty of Fort Laramie (1868). Prepare a 300–400-word analytical note with two source references, one explicit limitation and one proposed corroborating source.

Document workshop: Domestic transformation, international audiences; recognition and credibility memorandum. Identify emancipation’s scope and military context, and Fort Laramie’s territorial and governmental obligations; neither fully records implementation. Write a European cabinet memo on Confederate recognition at two wartime moments, considering military prospects, commercial interests, domestic opinion and emancipation. Explain separately why Fort Laramie raises a different recognition and credible-commitment question.

Teaching week 5 · Module 5 · Overseas Empire and Progressive-Era Power

The war of 1898 and its aftermath transformed the geographical reach of American power. Cuba, Puerto Rico, the Philippines, and Hawai‘i force students to distinguish annexation, military occupation, constrained sovereignty, and other forms of dependence.

Preparation: Read Annexation of Hawai‘i Resolution (1898) and Platt Amendment: 1903 treaty document. Prepare a 300–400-word analytical note with two source references, one explicit limitation and one proposed corroborating source.

Document workshop: A spectrum of empire; comparative sovereignty matrix. Map the Hawai‘i resolution’s and Platt Amendment’s powers, distinguishing annexation, sovereignty constraints and intervention. Compare Hawai‘i, Cuba and the Philippines by legal status, American objectives, local political agency, coercive instruments, legitimacy claims and source limits. Conclude why these are not identical forms of empire.

Teaching week 6 · Module 6 · World War I and the Limits of Wilsonian Order

World War I confronted the United States with a conflict in which commercial ties, maritime rights, security fears, and moral arguments became difficult to separate. Students reconstruct the movement from neutrality to the 1917 declaration of war without treating intervention as predetermined.

Preparation: Read War Message to Congress (1917) and Wilson’s Fourteen Points (1918). Prepare a 300–400-word analytical note with two source references, one explicit limitation and one proposed corroborating source.

Document workshop: War aims and peace commitments; a ratification hearing with evidence. Compare war-entry reasons with institutional and territorial proposals requiring others’ cooperation or acceptance. Role-play the administration, reservationist senators, League opponents and a colonised population. Each prepares a 200-word brief distinguishing documented positions from interpretation. Hold a hearing on one commitment; classify constitutional, strategic, partisan and unequal-application disputes.

Teaching week 7 · Module 7 · From Selective Engagement to Global War

The interwar United States was not simply absent from international affairs. Financial relationships, trade policy, arms limitation, and regional diplomacy connected it to the world even as many citizens resisted military commitments.

Preparation: Read Four Freedoms Address (1941) and Executive Order 9066 (1942). Prepare a 300–400-word analytical note with two source references, one explicit limitation and one proposed corroborating source.

Document workshop: Universal claims and exclusions; two documents, one wartime state. Compare a Four Freedoms promise with authority delegated by Executive Order 9066, without treating the order as a record of all later incarceration. Annotate both texts in two columns for rights, powers, audiences and absent people. Explain the coexistence of universal claims and discriminatory implementation, identifying additional evidence needed.

Teaching week 8 · Module 8 · Containment and the National Security State

The early Cold War reorganized American commitments and domestic institutions. Students trace the movement from wartime alliance to rivalry without assuming that every disagreement had the same cause or that confrontation was inevitable in its eventual form.

Preparation: Read “Truman Doctrine (1947)” and “Marshall Plan legislation (1948)”. Prepare a 300–400-word analytical note with two source references, one explicit limitation, and one proposed corroborating source.

Document workshop: Mechanism audit: assistance is not one kind of power. Compare the Truman Doctrine and Marshall Plan by threat, actor, recipient, target audience, intended behaviour and resources. Distinguish coercion, inducement, capacity-building and attraction. Specify evidence to test each mechanism and one possible unintended consequence.

Teaching week 9 · Module 9 · Nuclear Deterrence, Decolonization, and Credibility

Eisenhower’s presidency confronted the challenge of sustaining global commitments without unlimited conventional spending. Nuclear deterrence, alliances, covert action, and economic pressure offered different ways to manage that problem, each carrying risks that simple measures of military strength can conceal.

Preparation: Read “Executive Order 10730: Little Rock (1957)” and “Eisenhower’s Farewell Address (1961)”. Prepare a 300–400-word analytical note with two source references, one explicit limitation, and one proposed corroborating source.

Document workshop: Three audiences, three meanings of credibility. Compare Executive Order 10730’s legal and operational purpose with the farewell address’s account of defence institutions. Define credible conduct for a US budget official, an Egyptian nationalist and a Black student seeking integration. Identify sources for each perspective, assess whether one instrument could satisfy all three, and distinguish interpretation from documented fact and evidence of foreign perceptions.

Teaching week 10 · Module 10 · Flexible Response, Vietnam, and the Crisis of Consent

Kennedy and Johnson sought more flexible ways to compete than reliance on nuclear threats alone. The Peace Corps and development initiatives promised partnership and improvement, while counterinsurgency and conventional forces expanded the range of military options.

Preparation: Read “Executive Order Establishing the Peace Corps (1961)” and “Tonkin Gulf Resolution (1964)”. Prepare a 300–400-word analytical note with two source references, one explicit limitation, and one proposed corroborating source.

Document workshop: Build—and challenge—a theory of success. Compare objectives and authority in the Peace Corps order and Tonkin resolution; propose outcome measures and unintended consequences. For a development initiative or escalation decision, connect resources, action, immediate effects, political change and durable outcomes. Specify assumptions and evidence for each link; a second group identifies a failure point, an omitted local actor and an alternative explanation.

Teaching week 11 · Module 11 · Détente, Triangular Diplomacy, and Executive Limits

Nixon and Ford confronted war fatigue, economic strain, and a more complex distribution of international power. The opening to China and détente with the Soviet Union sought to exploit diplomatic opportunities rather than resolve ideological disagreement.

Preparation: Read “Shanghai Communiqué (1972), FRUS document 203” and “Ford’s Helsinki Address (1975), FRUS document 62”. Prepare a 300–400-word analytical note with two source references, one explicit limitation, and one proposed corroborating source.

Document workshop: Ambiguity and accountability workshop. Mark shared agreement, separate national positions and acknowledgment of another party’s position in the Shanghai Communiqué. Write a 200-word explanation of one ambiguity, its negotiating benefit and a possible cost to an affected third party. Compare Ford’s public Helsinki explanation and distinction between declarations and conduct. Do not invent confidential motives.

Teaching week 12 · Module 12 · Human Rights, Renewed Confrontation, and Negotiated Change

The late Cold War combined human-rights claims, regional intervention, renewed confrontation, and major diplomatic change. Carter’s emphasis on rights and the Camp David process must be considered alongside the Iranian revolution, hostage crisis, and Soviet intervention in Afghanistan.

Preparation: Read “Reagan’s Berlin Address: archival recording (1987)” and “INF Treaty (1987)”. Prepare a 300–400-word analytical note with two source references, one explicit limitation, and one proposed corroborating source.

Document workshop: Causal claims tribunal. Contrast the Berlin speech and INF Treaty by audience, commitments and verification implications. Assign teams to pressure, reassurance, domestic reform and popular mobilisation explanations. Each specifies a mechanism, supporting evidence and a weakening observation. Discuss what the recording and treaty can establish and what additional evidence is needed to claim that either caused later political transformation.

Teaching week 13 · Module 13 · The Unipolar Moment: Order, Markets, and Intervention

The disappearance of the Soviet Union left the United States with exceptional capabilities but no automatic agreement about how to use them. The Gulf War provided a case of coalition warfare with a defined territorial objective, while crises in Somalia, Rwanda, Bosnia, and Kosovo posed different questions about humanitarian responsibility, risk, and legitimacy.

Preparation: Read “Authorization for Force Against Iraq (1991)” and “National Security Strategy (1999)”. Prepare a 300–400-word analytical note with two source references, one explicit limitation, and one proposed corroborating source.

Document workshop: Intervention decision matrix. Compare the 1991 authorisation’s scope with the 1999 strategy’s broader aims. Contrast the Gulf War and one 1990s humanitarian crisis by objective, authorisation, partners, local actors, instruments, exit assumptions and civilian consequences. Mark cells requiring additional evidence rather than inference. Explain why success in one case does not automatically predict success in another.

Teaching week 13 · Module 14 · The War on Terror and the Search for Rebalance

The September 11 attacks transformed threat perception, domestic institutions, and the scope of authorized force. Afghanistan and Iraq require separate causal accounts: neither the adversaries nor the rationales for intervention were identical.

Preparation: Read “Authorization for Use of Military Force (2001)” and “Obama’s Statement on Iran (14 July 2015)”. Prepare a 300–400-word analytical note with two source references, one explicit limitation, and one proposed corroborating source.

Document workshop: Mechanism-focused policy memo. Identify who may act and against whom under the 2001 AUMF. Write a 500-word historical memo comparing that authorisation with Obama’s 2015 Iran announcement. Separate threat definition, domestic authorisation, instrument, promised outcome and testing evidence. Explain why sanctions and negotiation are not simply hard and soft opposites. Keep historical interpretation separate from current legal status.

Teaching week 14 · Module 15 · Competition, Interdependence, and Contested Leadership

The final week compares contemporary strategic arguments rather than presenting an unsettled period as a finished historical verdict. The 2017, 2022, and November 2025 National Security Strategies offer contrasting accounts of sovereignty, alliances, economic strength, and international order.

Preparation: Read “National Security Strategy (October 2022)” and “National Security Strategy (November 2025)”. Prepare a 300–400-word analytical note with two source references, one explicit limitation, and one proposed corroborating source.

Document workshop: Current-affairs evidence audit. Compare one category in the 2022 and November 2025 strategies; design a test using dated budgets or decisions. Select a 2026 development only after finding current sources. Tabulate fact, official claim, interpretation and prediction. Compare an earlier case, identify two analogy limits and evidence that would change the provisional conclusion. Do not treat strategy documents as implementation evidence.
