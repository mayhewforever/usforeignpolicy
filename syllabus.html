Aim and scope

This course develops historical explanations of U.S. foreign-policy choices through domestic politics, external constraints and instruments of power.

Founding, expansion, overseas empire, world wars, Cold War, post-Cold War order, the wars after 9/11 and contemporary competition. Each module connects institutions and public opinion with military, economic, diplomatic and cultural instruments.

Learning outcomes

  1. CLO1 / ÖÇ1. Explain turning points in U.S. foreign policy from the founding era to contemporary strategy.
  2. CLO2 / ÖÇ2. Analyze domestic institutions, elections, political economy, public opinion, race and ideas in foreign-policy choices.
  3. CLO3 / ÖÇ3. Compare the roles of international structures, allies, rivals and nonstate actors.
  4. CLO4 / ÖÇ4. Distinguish military coercion, economic statecraft, diplomacy and soft power.
  5. CLO5 / ÖÇ5. Evaluate primary documents and scholarly interpretations through source criticism.
  6. CLO6 / ÖÇ6. Construct an evidence-based argument about policy consequences.
  7. CLO7 / ÖÇ7. Defend a historical interpretation through respectful engagement with competing perspectives.

How to study

Start with the plain-language explanation and key terms. Continue to the detailed case analysis, compare an assigned source and try the question before opening its explained answer. Select a YouTube video or podcast for a second perspective. Additional media are optional, not extra graded work. Source titles are kept in their original language.

Methods: short explanatory lectures, document workshops, structured comparisons, small-group discussion and evidence-based writing. The reading room identifies primary documents, scholarship and media with access and purpose notes. Office of the Historian Milestones entries are retired historical background, not current-policy guidance.

Source use, integrity and accessibility

Cite evidence in APA 6 or later, distinguish quotations from paraphrases and acknowledge assistance. Explain source authorship, audience and limits. Institutional publicity is evidence of a stated position, not proof of success. Use the written lecture and primary documents when a media player or captions are unavailable; request an equivalent accessible format for required work. External player availability and caption accuracy have not been fully tested.
